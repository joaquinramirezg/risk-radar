How it works

For each company in the S&P 100 the two most recent 10-K filings are downloaded from SEC EDGAR. Item 1A is split into individual risk factors using the filing's own formatting: a risk factor's title is set in bold, italic or underline, on its own line or run into its first paragraph. A company is left out rather than shown wrong when either year yields fewer than eight risks. Each risk this year is matched to last year's with a small open embedding model (bge-small-en-v1.5) on its title and opening paragraph, one-to-one, at a similarity of 0.80 or more. A risk left without a partner is checked for reused sentences: if at least 10% of its five-word phrases appear verbatim in a risk on the other side, it was split off from that risk or merged into it, not added or dropped. Matched risks are then compared word by word: 98% or more the same is unchanged, 75% or more is edited, anything lower is rewritten.

Theme counts are plain phrase matches over the risk-factor text, so they can be checked with Find in the filing itself. A company that writes more pages will mention most things more often.

A theme counts as moving at a company when a phrase on it appears in words added this year, inside a risk that is new, split off, or whose edit the classifier called a new exposure or an escalation. Rewording that keeps a theme already there does not count. Each example on the front page is the sentence from this year's filing that contains the added phrase, and links to the risk with the full blackline.

How accurate it is

A seeded sample of 257 items from ten of the first 20 companies was checked: every risk called new or removed, plus 26 random matched pairs per company. Each item was judged by two AI labellers working independently (one on all 257, one on a 60-item core), not by a human. They agreed on 59 of the 60 core items (Cohen's κ 0.96).

Before the split check existed, 4 of the 15 risks called new or removed were really pieces of a risk that had been split or merged. Its 10% threshold was then set on this same sample, so those four are not an independent test; three further splits it found outside the sample were checked by reading them. The first version cut 14 of the 257 risks short. Thirteen were subsections of a longer risk in Microsoft's and Exxon Mobil's filings, read as risks of their own; the splitter now folds a short heading under a full-sentence title into that risk, and all thirteen are whole again. The fourteenth, a two-sentence Tesla risk about Elon Musk, really is that short in the filing.

All of that was measured on the first 20 companies, which the splitter and matcher were built on. The other 78 were then checked separately: an independent model (Claude Opus 5.5) read a fresh seeded sample from them and judged each call against the text. A model judge is not a person; this is a second reading of data the pipeline was never tuned on. Fifteen calls it could not settle from what it was shown, and two bodies it saw cut short by its own prompt limit, are left out.

Matching holds up on companies it was not built on; the weak spot is the rare risk with no partner. The audit first found Oracle's and AMD's risk-factor summaries read as risks; the splitter now drops a heading whose paragraphs are other risks' titles, which removed 12 such entries, 9 of them flagged by the judge (the table counts after that fix). Split and merge links are right only about two times in three on new filers: when a company renames the subject of a risk (Charter's Liberty Broadband deal became the Cox deal), the matcher sees a new risk carved out of an old one rather than the same risk rewritten. Read a split or merged tag as "related to", not "came from".

Edited and rewritten risks carry a second label from a language model that reads both versions and says what kind of change it was: new exposure, escalated, de-escalated or boilerplate. To see how good that can get, three model sizes were run on 80 changes labelled beforehand, scored on the 42 not used to write the instructions.

Against the original labels every model stopped at 62%, and the models agreed with each other (κ 0.56 to 0.82) far more than with the labels (κ 0.42 to 0.52). That pointed at the labels, which were written before the rule that a dropped example or a renamed regulator is boilerplate. The 17 changes where four or five of the five model runs agreed against the label were re-read against that rule, and 16 labels changed. Because only items the models agreed on were reviewed, the second column is an upper bound and the first a lower bound. Even so, the review is where the model sizes separate. Haiku is used here because it costs a thirtieth of Opus; treat its change tag as a pointer to where to look, not a verdict. Classifying all 1,527 changes across the 98 companies cost $0.70.

The design was compared with 34 alternatives on the same sample. A larger embedding model (bge-base) was no more accurate and three times slower. Plain TF-IDF word overlap came within two items of it, which says the task is mostly lexical. Matching on titles alone was 98% accurate. The sample was drawn from this design's own output, so the comparison favours it.
